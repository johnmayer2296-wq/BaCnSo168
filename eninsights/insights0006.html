What to Look for When Visiting a Chinese Factory: A Practical Checklist for Overseas Buyers

Many overseas buyers visit a Chinese factory for the first time without a clear idea of what to do. When you get there, you're not sure what to look for, what to ask, or how to tell if the factory is trustworthy—so you end up just walking through, seeing everything but understanding nothing.

This article cuts the fluff and gives you practical, step‑by‑step guidance. From the moment you step through the factory gate to the minute you leave, we lay out exactly what to inspect, whom to talk to, and what to record.

I. Before You Go: Do Your Homework—Don't Waste the Trip

Many people buy plane tickets and book hotels, only to find that the factory address is a room number in an office building. One phone call could have saved them the trouble.

Three things you must confirm before departure:

1. Verify the business license. Ask the supplier for a high‑resolution scan of their business license. Once you have it, log in to the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System (www.gsxt.gov.cn), enter the full company name or the unified social credit code, and check the following:

• Company name (the full Chinese name, not the English translation)

• Registered address (does it match the address you're visiting?)

• Business scope (does it include terms like "production" or "manufacturing," or only "sales" and "trade"?)

• Registered capital and paid‑in capital

• Date of establishment

• Operating status (check if it shows "active" and whether there are any abnormal records)

2. Confirm the factory address. If the address given is Suite 42 in a downtown office tower—cancel the trip. Real factories are in industrial zones or parks, not in CBD skyscrapers.

3. Plan your visit agenda. Make a checklist of what you want to see: quality control processes, production lines, equipment, and warehouse. Also prepare a list of questions so you don't miss anything important.

If the supplier refuses to provide a scanned business license or cooperate with basic verification before you travel—cancel the trip. Don't waste your airfare.

II. The First Glimpse at the Gate: Look at the "People"

The moment you step onto the factory floor, your observation begins.

Staff uniforms. Check whether employees wear uniform work clothes. A company that values teamwork usually requires uniforms—not just for appearances but as a reflection of management standards and team cohesion. If staff are dressed haphazardly, it suggests management is lax.

Employee morale. Are workers energetic and quick, or listless and slow? Someone who can't manage their own appearance is unlikely to do their job well.

Operational discipline. Observe whether workers perform their tasks correctly and skillfully. Improper operations indicate insufficient training, which means quality is out of control.

Who to talk to. Don't just chat with the salesperson. Try to speak with the production line supervisor, the quality manager, and the workshop director—they know the factory's real situation better than sales does. If you can exchange a few words with frontline workers, even better.

III. The Workshop: Five Things You Must Inspect

The workshop is the heart of the factory. Don't miss any of these five items.

1. Cleanliness and 5S Management

Is the workshop floor clean? Are tools and materials neatly arranged? The cleanliness of a workplace directly reflects management attitudes—a dirty, chaotic environment usually goes hand in hand with poor quality control.

Don't just look at the production area; common areas tell you even more. Take a look at the restrooms and the canteen. A factory that keeps its restrooms clean won't have terrible management.

Special warning: If the workshop is spotless like a showroom and the air is suspiciously fresh—especially in places like injection moulding or painting where odours are normal—chances are they aren't actually producing that day. You're looking at a carefully staged "display workshop."

2. Equipment

Quantity: Are there enough machines in the workshop? If only one or two small machines are running, where do the products come from? Likely they're subcontracted out—meaning quality and delivery schedules are out of your control.

Condition: Is there dust on the equipment? Are there maintenance logs and labels with responsible persons? I've heard of cases where equipment was covered in dust because it had been seized by the court over debt disputes—this is a major red flag.

Details: Ask about the brand, age, and maintenance record of the equipment. Newer machines show the factory is willing to invest. Ask technical staff about critical process parameters—if they can explain clearly, they have technical depth; if they're vague, trouble is likely ahead.

3. Raw Materials and Components

Check the raw material warehouse and the workshop floor: are raw materials and auxiliary supplies adequately stocked? Are they stored neatly and managed systematically?

Two key areas to check:

• Approved goods area: how are finished products stored and labelled?

• Non‑conforming goods area: where are defective items placed, are they clearly marked and separated, and how are they handled?

If defective and acceptable products are mixed together with no segregation—this is a huge red flag. If a factory can't even be bothered to separate rejects from good ones, can you trust them to control quality?

4. Quality Control

QC area: Is there a dedicated inspection zone with adequate lighting?

Testing equipment: Do they have callipers, gauges, functional testers—and are they being used or gathering dust?

QC records: Ask to see inspection reports from the past week. Records show they have a system; if they can't produce any, it's just talk.

Talk directly to the QC inspector: Find a quality inspector on site and have a brief chat. Ask if they have quality standards and process documents. If they can talk confidently, that's reassuring; if they stumble and mumble, raise a question mark.

5. Production Flow and Layout

Take time to understand the entire production workflow. Is the movement between processes smooth? Are there obvious bottlenecks? Is the layout logical?

If the whole flow looks chaotic and you can't get a clear explanation—this is a warning sign. A factory that can't organise its production line properly will struggle to deliver on time and to specification.

IV. Documents and Certifications: The Truth on Paper

Check the original business license. Ask to see the physical license on site and compare it with the scanned copy you received earlier.

Check certifications. ISO 9001, ISO 14001, BSCI, etc.—verify that the certificate holder name matches the entity you're dealing with. Also confirm product certifications (CE, FCC, RoHS, FDA, etc.).

Ask about export records. Which markets have they exported to recently? Which clients have they supplied? Specific answers indicate real export experience. If they refuse to share any client information, be cautious.

Check employee social insurance figures. A factory claiming annual revenue of tens of millions of yuan but with only two or three employees registered for social insurance—that doesn't add up.

V. Warehouses and Supply Chain: Hidden Truths

Raw material warehouse: Are materials sorted and stored with clear labels and protective measures? Is any material rusty or damp?

Finished goods area: Is packaging proper? Are there signs of water damage? Are labels clear?

Supply chain location: Is the factory close to its suppliers? If key raw materials have to be shipped from hundreds of kilometres away, supply chain risk is much higher.

VI. Ten Questions You Must Ask

During your visit, make sure to ask these questions:

01. What is your typical lead time?

02. Which processes or components are outsourced?

03. How many units can you produce per day/week?

04. What quality control measures do you have in place?

05. How do you handle defective products?

06. Can you provide references from other clients?

07. What percentage of workers returned after Chinese New Year?

08. What is your monthly employee turnover rate?

09. Apart from sales, who will follow up on our orders, and how long have they been with the factory?

10. If order volume suddenly increases by 30%, can you handle it?

VII. Red Flags You Must Watch Out For

If you see any of the following, reconsider whether to proceed with cooperation:

• Quotation is 20% or more lower than market price—they've probably cut corners somewhere you can't see.

• Factory documents are incomplete or cannot be verified.

• Registered address is an office building, not an industrial zone.

• Refusal to provide a scanned copy of the business license.

• Refusal to show the workshop or only showing the sample room.

• Workshop is "too clean" and doesn't look like normal production.

• Equipment is covered in dust or clearly not in use.

• Defective and acceptable products are mixed without segregation.

• QC staff cannot describe quality standards or processes.

• Workshop layout is chaotic and production flow is unclear.

VIII. After the Visit: Do Two Things Immediately

First, record everything. That evening, write down every detail you saw and heard. Memory fades quickly, and you'll miss key points if you wait a few days.

Second, cross‑check. Compare notes with colleagues who visited with you. Different people notice different things, and combining perspectives gives you a more complete picture.

Conclusion

Visiting a factory in China boils down to one core idea: You're not there to make friends—you're there to find problems.

If you spend four hours on a visit and don't find a single issue, you probably didn't look carefully enough. No properly functioning factory is perfect.

Before the visit: verify the business license, confirm the address, do your homework.

During the visit: inspect people, equipment, materials, QC, processes, documentation, and ask questions.

After the visit: record, cross‑check, and make a balanced judgment.

This approach is certainly more work than a casual walk‑through. But one serious factory visit might save you hundreds of thousands—or even millions—of dollars in losses. That time is well spent.
