Shipping from China: Basic Guide for Overseas Buyers

Many overseas buyers spend 90% of their effort on finding factories and negotiating prices, leaving only the final 10% for logistics — and that's exactly where things go wrong.


Your goods are produced, but how do you get them out? How much will it cost? How long will it take? What documents are needed? These questions, if left until the goods are ready, often come too late. Freight costs double your estimate, you can't secure a vessel booking, or customs holds your shipment due to incomplete documentation — every pitfall means real financial loss.

This article skips the theory and focuses on practical steps. From the moment you place your order to the arrival of your goods at the destination port, here's exactly what to do and what to watch out for at every stage.

1. Choosing a Shipping Method: Four Options, Each with Its Purpose

There are four main shipping methods from China. The core logic for choosing is: first consider your time requirements, then your shipment size, and finally calculate cost.

Ocean Freight — The Choice for Bulk Goods

Ocean freight is the lowest‑cost option, with rates per kilogram or cubic meter at just one‑fifth to one‑tenth of air freight. It has no upper limit on volume and can accommodate oversized or overweight cargo. It is ideal for large‑volume, non‑urgent shipments.

However, ocean freight is also the slowest, with main trade lanes taking 15 to 45 days. The service chain is longer, requiring you to coordinate with freight forwarders and customs brokers, involving multiple steps such as booking, customs clearance, and pickup.

As of 2026 data, a 40‑foot full container from China to the US West Coast costs approximately $2,500 to $3,800. LCL (Less than Container Load) costs around $80 to $120 per cubic meter.

Air Freight — The Choice for Urgent Goods

Air freight is fast, typically taking 3 to 7 days, with direct flights cutting that to 2 to 4 days. It costs 5 to 10 times more than sea freight but is 30% to 50% cheaper than international express couriers. It suits medium‑urgency orders, high‑value products, or restocking needs.

Air freight has limited capacity — single shipments usually don't exceed 1,000 kg — and has clear restrictions on product dimensions and categories (e.g., liquids, batteries).

International Express — The Best Solution for Samples and Small Packages

International express (DHL, UPS, FedEx) offers the fastest transit — door‑to‑door in 1 to 5 days — with full tracking and efficient customs clearance.

The downside is also clear: highest cost. Single shipments are typically no more than 30 kg. It is ideal for documents, samples, and small urgent orders.

China‑Europe Railway — A Mid‑Range Option for Europe

If you're shipping to Europe, the China‑Europe Railway is a solid alternative. Transit time: 18 to 25 days — faster than sea, cheaper than air. Cost is about $250 to $350 per cubic meter. It suits medium‑weight cargo with moderate time sensitivity.

2. Two Key Concepts: FCL and LCL

In ocean freight, two terms must be understood: FCL (Full Container Load) and LCL (Less than Container Load).

FCL: You rent an entire container exclusively for your cargo. Suitable for large volumes (typically over 15 cubic meters). Advantages: faster transit (no waiting to consolidate), better cargo safety (your goods only), and lower damage rates.

LCL: Your cargo shares a container with other shippers' goods. Suitable for smaller quantities, charged by volume (per cubic meter). Downsides: longer transit times (waiting for the container to fill before sailing), and LCL rates are 20% to 30% more expensive per unit compared to FCL.

How to choose: If your cargo fills more than half a container, use FCL. If it's just a few cubic meters, use LCL.

3. What Do FOB, EXW, CIF, and DDP Mean?

Incoterms determine who pays freight, who buys insurance, who handles customs clearance, and at which point risk transfers. The four most commonly used are:

EXW (Ex Works)

The seller only makes the goods available at their factory. The buyer arranges everything from loading, transport, export clearance, to import clearance.

Suitable for experienced buyers with local support in China. Not recommended for beginners, as everything is on your side.

FOB (Free on Board)

The seller delivers goods to the named port of shipment and loads them on board, covering export clearance. The buyer covers international ocean freight, insurance, and import clearance.

FOB is the most commonly used term by overseas buyers. Note: FOB applies only to sea freight.

CIF (Cost, Insurance, and Freight)

The seller covers the cost of goods, international freight, and basic insurance. The buyer covers import clearance and duties.

Choose CIF if you don't want to arrange international transport and insurance yourself. CIF applies only to sea freight. Note: For air freight, FOB and CIF do not apply — use FCA, CPT, or CIP instead.

DDP (Delivered Duty Paid)

The seller bears all costs — from factory to buyer's doorstep — including transport, insurance, export clearance, import clearance, and duties.

Most hassle‑free for the buyer, but the seller's quote will include all costs. Ideal for buyers who don't want to handle any logistics details.

4. Essential Documents: Missing One Can Delay Everything

When shipping from China, the following documents are standard.

Commercial Invoice

Lists product name, quantity, unit price, total value, buyer and seller information, and trade terms. Declared value must match the actual goods — undervaluing is a violation.

Packing List

Lists gross weight, net weight, volume, packaging method, and carton numbers for each box. Ensure it matches the actual goods with less than 5% variance.

Bill of Lading (B/L)

The ocean Bill of Lading is a document of title — whoever holds it, owns the goods. Information must exactly match the customs declaration.

Export Customs Declaration

Filed online through China's Single Window system. The HS code must be accurate — errors can lead to rejection or inspection.

Other Potentially Required Documents

Depending on your product category, you may also need: Certificate of Origin, product certifications (CE, UL, FDA, etc.), MSDS (for chemicals), and fumigation certificate (for wooden packaging).

Golden rule: all key information across documents must be perfectly consistent — product name, quantity, weight, amount. If the invoice and packing list don't match, customs will hold your shipment.

5. Freight Forwarders: Do You Need One? How to Find One?

Do you need one? Absolutely yes.

Shipping from China involves multiple steps — booking, customs clearance, trucking, warehousing, import clearance, and more. A professional freight forwarder can help you with three things:

Choose the right shipping route and method

Handle customs documentation and processes

Secure lower freight rates than you can get on your own

How to find a reliable freight forwarder?

Check credentials: Confirm they have a valid business license and NVOCC (Non‑Vessel Operating Common Carrier) registration.

Check experience: Ask which routes and product categories they have handled. A forwarder experienced with your target market and product type knows which pitfalls to avoid.

Check quotation structure: Ask for a detailed fee breakdown — ocean freight, documentation fees, customs clearance fees, trucking fees, destination charges — rather than a lump‑sum total.

Check response speed: Send an inquiry and see how long they take to reply and whether the response is professional. A forwarder who is slow to reply to an inquiry will be even less responsive when problems arise.

A practical benchmark: A good forwarder will proactively ask about your product name, HS code, and whether it contains batteries or liquids — because these details directly affect what is shippable and how. Those who quote based only on weight and volume are less professional.

6. How to Calculate Costs: Don't Just Look at Freight

The total cost of shipping from China is not just the "ocean freight" or "air freight" line item.

Full cost breakdown includes:

Inland charges: trucking from factory to port, export customs clearance

International freight: ocean/air/express charges

Insurance: 0.3% to 0.5% of cargo value

Destination charges: import clearance, duties, VAT, port fees

Last‑mile delivery: from destination port to final warehouse

Hidden costs often overlooked:

Bunker Adjustment Factor (BAF): fluctuates with oil prices

Peak season surcharge: before Chinese New Year and Christmas, rates can jump 20% to 50%

Storage charges: daily fees apply if cargo stays at the port beyond the free period

Inspection fees: if customs selects your shipment for examination, additional time and costs arise

Ways to save: plan early to avoid peak seasons, consolidate multiple orders into one shipment, and optimize packaging to reduce volume.

7. Common Problems and How to Avoid Them

Problem 1: Customs Holds Your Shipment
The most common reason is documentation issues — vague product descriptions, under‑declared value, weight discrepancies, or incorrect HS codes. If documents are incomplete or inconsistent, customs will hold your cargo.
Solution: Double‑check all documents before shipment, ensuring invoice, packing list, and Bill of Lading match exactly. If unsure about an HS code, consult your freight forwarder or a professional.


Problem 2: Shipment Delays
Delays can result from port congestion, weather, schedule changes, or customs inspections. But the most common cause is insufficient lead time planning.
Solution: Allow at least 15 to 30 days of buffer beyond your planned shelf‑date. Don't cut shipping deadlines too tight.


Problem 3: Freight Costs Double Your Expectation
This happens when you only asked for the "ocean freight" rate, not the all‑inclusive price. Many hidden charges — documentation fees, customs fees, trucking charges, destination port fees — are only revealed later.
Solution: Always request an all‑inclusive quote with a full breakdown before placing the order.


Problem 4: Damaged Goods
The cause is usually insufficient packaging. During sea transit, containers shake and stack, and poor packaging leads to damage.
Solution: Confirm export‑grade packaging standards with your factory. For fragile items, request wooden crates or foam reinforcement. Ask your forwarder to take photos as proof.

8. Timeline: From Order to Delivery, Step by Step

• Here is a typical ocean freight timeline (China to the US as an example):

• Factory production complete: depends on order size

• Booking + trucking to port: 3‑5 days

• Export customs clearance: 1‑2 days

• Ocean transit: 25‑35 days

• Destination port clearance: 3‑7 days

• Last‑mile delivery: 2‑5 days

• Total: approximately 35‑50 days

Air freight is significantly shorter: booking 1‑2 days, transit 5‑8 days, clearance 2‑3 days — total about 10‑14 days.

Summary

Shipping from China comes down to three things: choosing the right method, having the right documents, and finding the right forwarder. Pick your shipping method based on lead time and cargo volume; understand which trade term defines who pays for what; ensure all documents — invoice, packing list, and Bill of Lading — match perfectly; and choose a forwarder with experience and transparent quoting. Get these four pieces right, and logistics won't be the bottleneck in your sourcing journey.
