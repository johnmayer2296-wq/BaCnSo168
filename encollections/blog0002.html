Twelve Zodiac Pendant Charm | Auspicious Keepsake from the Ancient Eastern Cycle of Time

In ancient Eastern China, people observed the turning of the sun and moon and the cycle of seasons. They discovered that natural rhythms complete a full cycle approximately every twelve years. So they selected twelve representative creatures and established the Chinese zodiac system — a timekeeping tradition passed down for millennia, and a deeply poetic symbol within classical Eastern culture.

Twelve animals correspond to twelve years. Each birth year is matched with a specific zodiac sign, and the cycle repeats every twelve years — much like the changing seasons and the movement of the stars — orderly yet full of life. This is precisely why the Chinese zodiac resonates across borders and cultures: marking time through living creatures is a universal expression of human imagination.

Many people wonder: why did the ancients ultimately choose the rat, ox, tiger, rabbit, dragon, snake, horse, goat, monkey, rooster, dog, and pig — rather than other animals?

This selection was not random. It came from thousands of years of careful observation of nature and daily life. The ancients divided day and night into twelve two-hour periods and assigned each animal according to its most active hours — including domestic animals, wild beasts, and even a mythical creature — reflecting the full spectrum of life.

Rat (Zi): Deep at night, when all is still, rats are most active — the liveliest creatures in the dark hours.

Ox (Chou): In the early hours, oxen chew their cud. As farming's most vital partner, they embody cultivation and endurance.

Tiger (Yin): At dawn, tigers roam and hunt — the majestic sovereign of the wild.

Rabbit (Mao): At daybreak, rabbits emerge to forage. Gentle and calm, they echo the peaceful light of morning.

Dragon (Chen): As mist rises and rain falls, the mythical dragon was believed to govern clouds and rain — the only legendary creature among the twelve.

Snake (Si): As temperatures rise mid-morning, snakes emerge from burrows, sensing the warmth of the earth.

Horse (Wu): At high noon, horses gallop with vigor — symbolizing life's boundless vitality.

Goat (Wei): In the afternoon, when grass is freshest, goats graze leisurely — thriving on nature's abundance.

Monkey (Shen): As afternoon heat fades, monkeys play and chatter in the forest — lively, clever, and full of spirit.

Rooster (You): At dusk, roosters return to their coops. The ancients relied on their calls to tell the time of day.

Dog (Xu): In the evening, as homes light up, dogs stand guard — protecting the peace of the night.

Pig (Hai): Late at night, pigs sleep soundly — representing comfort, contentment, and a life of ease.

Beyond this, there is a deeper philosophical layer: these twelve creatures include birds, beasts, wild animals, domestic animals, and mythical beings — each with a distinct temperament. Some are gentle, some brave, some clever, some calm. Together, they mirror the diversity of human nature. The ancients thus reminded us: every being has its unique gift, and every person has their own strengths.

Note: The folk tale of the zodiac race is a beloved legend; the timing-based observation is more widely accepted by scholars as the historical origin.
